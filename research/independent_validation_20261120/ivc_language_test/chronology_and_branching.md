# Chronology, subgrouping and what the Indus signs add

This is a targeted follow-up to the vocabulary portability analysis, not an exhaustive dating of every root or meaning. The comparison established modern dictionary attestations and existing etymological groupings. It did not establish that each proposed sound, specialized sense and rebus pairing existed together during the Mature Harappan period, approximately 2600–1900 BCE.

## Chronological flags in the actual proposed readings

| Sign / proposal | Directly checked evidence | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| `142` staff → smith, *kōl/kol* | DEDR 2133 gives Tamil *kol* an iron-working sense, *kollaṉ* blacksmith; Malayalam includes artificer, while Kannada/Telugu include furnace terms. | An explicitly iron-working interpretation needs an archaeological and linguistic dating argument. A generic metalworker/furnace interpretation could be older, but cannot be assumed merely to rescue the exact iron gloss. The word family's age and the iron sense's age are separate questions. |
| `503` mountain → garland | DEDR 4827 explicitly classifies Kannada *māle* as a tatsama, a Sanskrit loan. | This Kannada item is not evidence for inherited Proto-Dravidian garland vocabulary. The earlier script missed the label because it was in the gloss rather than metadata. Corrected now. This label does not establish the borrowing direction or age of Tamil *mālai* or every other language's word. |
| `575` *kōṭṭai* capacity measure; `320` *paṭi* measure/ration | The cited dictionary defines particular regional measures through marakkāl, palam, ollock and seer equivalents. | These equations do not independently establish Harappan capacities or their names. A generic measure or allowance sense could be old; the exact historical unit and conversion need their own evidence. |
| `861` eye/sight → weight/property | DEDR 1444 includes weight, landholding and fraction senses; some language definitions use later coinage, including an anna fraction. | The dictionary's explanations must not be projected wholesale into Harappan institutions. This is not evidence that the sign reads anna; the original proposal uses *kāṇam/kāṇi*. Ancient use of those particular sounds and specialized meanings remains to be shown. |
| `520` spear → chief, *vēl/vēḷ* | DEDR 5545 includes generic ruler meanings alongside specifically historical Chalukya and Vēḷāḷa references. | The specific later dynastic/caste identifications are not Harappan evidence. The general chief word could be older; those later definitions alone neither date nor falsify it. |

The relevant [archaeometallurgical review by Kenoyer and Miller](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1999_Metal%20Technologies%20of%20the%20Indus%20Valley%20Tradition.pdf), printed p. 113, reports no confirmed iron objects from Harappan Phase sites in the greater Indus region, while documenting copper and copper alloys. That is a reason to flag an iron-specific gloss, not to claim that every metalworking word must be late or that iron nowhere existed in South Asia.

The basic concepts—eye, fish, horn, small numerals—have no comparable cultural anachronism apparent from their meanings. Nevertheless, an old concept does not itself date a specific word, a secondary meaning, or the homophony required for a rebus. Conversely, a late written attestation does not prove that a word was newly invented then. We have not established an unambiguous late-origin word whose actual occurrence in an independently read Indus inscription falsifies the proposal.

Modern Brahui replacements such as Farsi-marked *xāna* house and *kamān* bow must also be kept in their proper role: they are comparison vocabulary, **not sound values assigned to the signs by this key**. Their presence does not, by itself, make the original key anachronistic.

## Are we recovering the branching of Dravidian?

We did **not infer a phylogenetic tree from the Indus signs**. The matrix locates proposed readings within pre-existing dictionary families. Shared vocabulary can reflect ancestral retention, later loss, contact, or incomplete documentation. Shared innovations and regular sound correspondences are more discriminating for subgrouping than raw overlap.

The horn family's Tamil–Brahui–Kurukh–Malto distribution therefore does not establish a special Tamil–northern branch. It could be an older retention surviving in separated languages. Likewise, the Venn display pools geographical comparison groups, not evolutionary clades.

Kurukh–Malto is a recognized close relationship; grouping it with Brahui into a single North Dravidian clade has been debated. [Kobayashi 2020](https://gengo.l.u-tokyo.ac.jp/masatok/JAOS_140_2_Kobayashi.pdf), pp. 467–468, discusses the distinction and proposed sound changes. Our controlled root inventory finds no overall northern excess, and it supplies no new shared innovation resolving that debate.

Family chronology also cannot be read off the sign matches. [Kolipakam et al. 2018](https://simon.net.nz/articles/a-bayesian-phylogenetic-study-of-the-dravidian-language-family/) estimate an approximately 4,500-year age for the sampled family, with uncertainty in relationships among its main branches. Such model-based dating is relevant context, not proof of the Indus language or of a particular administrative sense at Harappa.

## What is new, and what was already known?

**Most linguistic connections used here were already known.** Fish/star comparisons, northern eye cognates, the horn group, sprout/young connections and the other DEDR groups were not discovered from these signs by this analysis. The new work checks which proposed connections actually survive within individual languages, separates retained roots from retained senses, exposes coverage and selection effects, and identifies errors such as the loan label above.

At present the signs have not supplied a securely dated new Dravidian word, established a new cognate family, or changed the family tree. Their phonetic values and referents are still hypotheses, so using them to revise the same linguistic evidence that generated the readings would be circular.

An independently validated decipherment could change this: a dated inscription could establish an early word form or specialized sense, demonstrate that two sounds were already merged or still distinct, or place a shared grammatical innovation before a known date. To do that, the signs' reading must succeed on independent evidence first. The current contribution is a more testable set of candidates and exclusions, not that historical-linguistic result.
